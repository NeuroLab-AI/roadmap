Evidence-Led Systems for CNS Drug Discovery

Public Roadmap

NeuroLab reads a chemical structure and traces its likely path through the brain — targets, cell populations, circuits, regions, networks, cognition. Every claim is traceable to its source, and the system is built to say “we don’t know” rather than guess.

Quarters are sequential phases, not calendar dates. Each phase commits to a published artifact, not to a research outcome — results depend on what the evidence turns out to say; what we control is whether we publish it, and how honestly.

What makes it different

Two things separate this from a target predictor

A whole-brain map, not a target list

Most computational pharmacology stops at the target: this compound binds that receptor. NeuroLab carries the consequence outward — onto a whole-brain representation of several hundred regions, with the neuromodulatory nuclei, circuits and pathways that connect them.

The output is not a list of proteins. It is a map of where in the brain a compound is likely to act, and through which route it got there.

Reasoning you can follow, not a black box

Between the molecule and the map sits a mechanistic reasoning layer that composes the path one step at a time — receptor to cell type to circuit to region to network to cognition. Each step is a stated, inspectable claim, so a conclusion can be examined link by link and rejected at whichever link fails.

A neural network that outputs a ranked list cannot be argued with. A composed mechanistic path can.

The year ahead — four phases

Q1

Evidence you can audit

Most platforms ask you to trust their data. We are going to publish ours.

Today The knowledge base carries compound–target evidence with provenance attached, and reports show the sources behind them. What it does not yet do is publish a measured account of how accurate that evidence is, or guarantee that the evidence shown and the data trained on can never disagree.

  • A public data-quality standard. We measure the precision of our compound–target evidence — including direction, the difference between a compound switching a target on and switching it off — and publish the measurement rather than asserting the result. An agonist switches a target on; an antagonist switches it off. The distinction is the difference between predicting an effect and predicting its opposite, so we audit direction systematically — starting with the cases a machine can settle without human review, which makes high-precision coverage affordable at scale.
  • Citations that always resolve. Every identifier we publish points to a real, checkable source. A fabricated reference is a build failure, not a warning. AI systems are known to invent plausible-looking references. Identifiers are resolved against source databases, and anything that does not resolve is removed rather than published. An empty citation list is an acceptable outcome; an invented one is not.
  • One source of truth. The evidence we show and the models we train speak with a single voice, by construction — one resolved record behind both. The two views have different jobs, so they legitimately differ in what they include. What they must never do is describe the same fact in opposite terms. Both are consolidated onto a single resolved record, so that class of contradiction cannot arise — enforced by construction rather than by inspection.
  • Pharmacology with context. Compounds whose effect depends on tissue or on the state of the system are represented as such, rather than flattened into a single yes-or-no answer. A partial agonist can behave as an activator when a system is quiet and as a blocker when it is already active; some drugs act one way in one tissue and the opposite way in another. This is real pharmacology, and a yes-or-no field cannot express it.
  • Mechanism with explicit structure. Every step in a mechanistic path carries its own type, direction and evidence, so a chain of reasoning can be inspected link by link rather than taken on trust. Two things follow. A reader can reject a conclusion at the specific step that fails, rather than accepting or dismissing the whole chain. And a step established once becomes reusable everywhere it applies, so mechanistic coverage compounds instead of being rebuilt per compound.
  • Evaluation that holds still. Versioned, frozen evaluation sets, so progress is comparable over time and improvements are real. With the held-out portion independently reconstructable, so a result from six months ago means the same thing today and improvement is never an artefact of a changed test.

Delivered asA published data-quality report, a unified versioned knowledge base, and a typed mechanistic representation underneath the reasoning layer.

Q2

Evidence at scale

The bottleneck in computational neuroscience is not models. It is evidence with its context intact.

Today Evidence reaches the knowledge base largely through structured databases and hand curation. That produces high precision and slow growth, and it means the primary literature — where the context lives — is only partly reachable.

  • A literature-to-evidence pipeline. Primary studies become structured observations that keep what makes them meaningful — species, dose, route, comparator, endpoint, effect size, uncertainty, and the negative results that most pipelines discard. Negative results are routinely discarded and are often the most informative records in the set.
  • A pipeline that reports its own accuracy. We publish how well the extraction performs, including how often two expert reviewers disagree with each other. Automated extraction accelerates the processing of candidate evidence; it does not by itself make an observation scientifically admissible. So the pipeline is evaluated like any other component, and those numbers are published alongside the capability.
  • Deeper pharmacology. Dose, exposure, selectivity, metabolites, and documented disagreements between studies — the context that separates a mechanism that is plausible in a brain from one that is merely possible in an assay.
  • Performance reported per capability. Targets, brain regions, cognition, indications and side effects are not equally hard and are not equally well evidenced. Each is reported separately, with a model card stating what it is ready for. A single accuracy number for a system that predicts all of those would be meaningless, and averaging them hides exactly what a researcher needs to know.
  • Knowing when to stop. The most useful thing a research tool can say is often that the evidence does not support an answer. We measure how often NeuroLab declines — and whether the cases it declines are the ones it would otherwise have got wrong. Abstention is only a virtue if it is aimed well; a system that refuses at random is merely unhelpful. Note the distinction we hold to: a statement that a compound falls outside the system’s applicable range is a description of scope, not a calibrated probability that an answer is correct.
  • Reports that reproduce. A saved analysis carries its inputs, its settings, and the versions of every model and evidence source behind it, so it can be re-run, cited, shared, and challenged. Reproducible execution is not the same as scientific correctness — it means a result can be examined rather than merely trusted, which is the precondition for anyone challenging it.

Delivered asAn evidence-acquisition pipeline with published accuracy, a public evaluation page, a model card, and reproducible citable reports.

Q3

NeuroLab in your pipeline

Discovery work is already in motion. NeuroLab should slot into it, not interrupt it.

Today NeuroLab analyses one small molecule at a time, submitted as a chemical structure. The API exists and is richly featured, but it is not publicly documented or version-guaranteed, and there is no free way to try the system.

  • Models trained on the deepened evidence base, with the before-and-after published. If the gain is smaller than expected, that is published too — a stronger evidence base that does not move the numbers is itself an important finding about where the real limits sit, and it is the kind of result that usually goes unreported.
  • Novel structures. Analysis of compounds not yet in the knowledge base, working from chemical structure — with candidate targets and the experiments that would distinguish between them.
  • Beyond small molecules: peptides. Chemical fingerprints describe a small molecule well and a peptide poorly, so peptide drugs are currently under-served by the structure route. This phase adds a sequence-aware path for peptides and related biologics. Two things keep this honest. Coverage is not the same as prediction: a peptide can be represented, carry its documented effects and evidence, and still have no target we are willing to name. And that is frequently the correct answer rather than a gap — a large share of the peptides we have curated do not act through a single characterised receptor. A system that invents a plausible receptor for every peptide would score better on coverage and be worse at science.
  • Reasoning that keeps its alternatives. When the evidence supports more than one mechanism, NeuroLab preserves the competing branches and names the experiment that would separate them, instead of collapsing to a single confident answer. For a researcher deciding which experiment to run next, the branch point is the useful output, and collapsing it destroys the information.
  • Whole compound sets, side by side. Where they agree, where they diverge, and which differences the evidence actually supports.
  • A free exploration tier. Anyone can inspect the brain map and the knowledge graph.
  • A documented, versioned API, so NeuroLab can be called as a deterministic step inside pipelines that are already running — including by AI agents that need a citable answer rather than a generated one. A cold start takes roughly nine seconds. Warm responses range from well under a second to a few seconds depending on the analysis requested. Holding a warm-path budget as the system grows richer is part of this phase.

Delivered asRetrained models with published evaluation, a sequence-aware path for peptides, comparison across compound sets, public API documentation, and a free tier anyone can use.

Q4

Mechanism, proven

Depth we can defend, tested by people who are not us.

Today Mechanistic paths are composed across a whole-brain map, and outputs carry uncertainty and abstention. What has not happened yet is publishing a brain system together with its full adversarial test record, or putting predictions in front of an external group.

  • Validated mechanism modules. A focused set of brain systems tested exhaustively — including against deliberately wrong inputs — and published with their failures alongside their successes. Wrong compound, wrong dose, wrong species, wrong task — to confirm the system fails when it should. Each module passes its own gate before it is reused. Validating one does not validate the others, and we say so plainly.
  • Anatomy with resolution. Cell types, cortical layers, and directed pathways between regions, so a hypothesis can say where, and in which population, an effect is proposed to occur. Directed anatomy also works as a filter rather than only a ranking: a route that no known pathway supports can be excluded outright, which is often more valuable than another plausible-looking suggestion.
  • Persistent research workspaces. Repeated analyses accumulate into a queryable body of work instead of disappearing after each session.
  • Independent validation, on a published standard. We publish the protocol — predefined endpoints, comparators, and a commitment to report results that do not support our predictions — and begin external studies against it. This depends on external partners and on studies that take time, so we separate what we control from what we do not. External results complete when the science completes, and we will not describe them as finished before they are.

Delivered asValidated mechanism modules, an expanded anatomical layer, research workspaces, a public validation protocol, and external studies underway.

Beyond the plan

Where the research is heading

These are directions, not commitments. They are separated from the four phases above on purpose: each depends on a scientific method that is still open, and we will not attach a date to work whose approach is not yet settled. They are listed because they explain where the platform is pointed.

Reading the brain backwards

Today NeuroLab reasons forward — from a molecule to the effects it is likely to produce. The inverse problem is harder and considerably more valuable: given an observed brain signature, which mechanisms could have produced it, which of them are genuinely indistinguishable on the current evidence, and what experiment would separate them?

From molecule to distributed network

Carrying a molecular action all the way outward across the whole-brain map — through the cell populations expressing the target, the circuits they sit in, and the directed pathways connecting them — to the distributed networks the compound is likely to reach, with the route shown rather than asserted.

Measurements as lenses, not truths

PET, EEG, fMRI, electrophysiology and behaviour do not measure the same underlying thing, and treating them as interchangeable signals is one of the quieter ways computational neuroscience goes wrong. The direction here is an explicit model of what each method actually observes.

Denser mechanistic coverage

Extending validated mechanistic depth across the major neuromodulatory and cognitive systems — cholinergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic, dopaminergic, thalamocortical and beyond — one validated system at a time rather than by asserting whole-brain coverage.

Standards

The lines we don’t cross

Stated up front, because in this field the omissions are where the overclaiming happens.

  1. Binding to a target is not the same as producing a brain effect, and we never present it as such.
  2. A visualized brain map is a hypothesis about where an effect may occur, not a measurement of that compound’s effect.
  3. Our reasoning layer composes mechanistic pathways. It is not a validated simulation of causation.
  4. Development results are not independent validation, and we label them accordingly.
  5. We do not describe prospective validation as existing until the studies are complete.
